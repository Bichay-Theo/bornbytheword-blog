## Carson's Response
**Prompt:** Exegete 2 Corinthians 5:19-21. Focus specifically on the meaning of 'katallasso' (reconciliation) and the 'Great Exchange' in verse 21. Does the text imply that God's own wrath was propitiated in this reconciliation, and how does imputation work here?

An exegetical analysis of 2 Corinthians 5:19–21 reveals the heart of Pauline soteriology, demonstrating how God’s justice and mercy kiss at the cross. To grasp the mechanics of this text, we must examine the semantic and theological contours of reconciliation (*katallasso*), the necessity of propitiation, and the structural symmetry of the "Great Exchange" (v. 21) regarding imputation.

---

### 1. The Exegesis of *Katallasso* (Reconciliation) in 5:19–20

The Greek verb *katallasso* (and its cognate noun *katallage*) lexically denotes the restoration of a broken relationship, changing from enmity to peace. In the wider Greco-Roman world, reconciliation was typically initiated by the offending party seeking to appease the offended party. 

In Paul’s theology, however, there is a striking reversal:
* **God is the initiator:** "God was in Christ reconciling the world to himself" (v. 19). It is not humanity seeking to appease an capricious deity; it is the offended Creator who sets the initiative to restore the cosmos.
* **The barrier is objective:** Reconciliation is not merely a subjective change in human attitude (i.e., God coaxing hostile humans to stop being angry at Him). Rather, it requires the objective removal of the cause of enmity—namely, human sin. Paul links the act of reconciliation directly to the legal status of transgressions: "...not counting their trespasses against them" (*me logizomenos autois ta paraptomata auton*). 
* **The dual aspect of reconciliation:** Verse 19 describes an *accomplished objective reality* (God has reconciled the world through Christ’s death), while verse 20 issues a *subjective appeal* ("Be reconciled to God"). The historical, accomplished work of Christ must be personally appropriated through faith.

---

### 2. Is God’s Wrath Propitiated in this Reconciliation?

To ask whether God’s own wrath was propitiated in this act of reconciliation is to ask about the theological foundation of *katallasso*. While the term *katallasso* itself focuses on the *result* (peace/restored relationship), it logically and exegetically presupposes the removal of the *obstacle* causing the enmity—which is God's holy and judicial wrath against sin.

* **The Reality of Divine Wrath:** For Paul, God’s wrath (*orge*) is not an irrational burst of anger, but His holy, settled, judicial opposition to evil (cf. Rom 1:18, 2:5). 
* **Reconciliation via Propitiation:** In Romans 5:9–10, Paul explicitly links salvation from God’s wrath with reconciliation: *"Since, therefore, we have now been justified by his blood, much more shall we be saved by him from the wrath of God. For if while we were enemies we were reconciled to God by the death of his Son..."* 
* **The Mechanism in 2 Cor 5:19 & 21:** God reconciles the world to Himself *"not counting"* their trespasses. How can a just God simply "not count" sins? The answer is given in verse 21: because He counted them to Christ instead. By making the sinless One to "be sin for us," God poured out His judicial judgment on the substitute. Therefore, while *katallasso* is the overarching framework of restored relationship, it is grounded in a *propitiatory* sacrifice—wherein God’s righteous wrath against sin was fully satisfied (propitiated) at the cross, allowing His love to flow to the offender without compromising His justice (cf. Rom 3:25–26).

---

### 3. The "Great Exchange" and Imputation in 2 Corinthians 5:21

Verse 21 is perhaps the most concentrated statement of the substitutionary atonement in the New Testament: 
> *"For our sake he made him to be sin who knew no sin, so that in him we might become the righteousness of God."*

To understand how imputation functions here, we must analyze the two parallel halves of this verse.

#### A. "He made him to be sin who knew no sin" (The Imputation of Sin)
* **The Sinless Substitute:** "Him who knew no sin" refers to the impeccable moral purity of Jesus Christ. He was entirely without personal sin, making Him the qualified sacrificial Lamb.
* **"Made Sin" (*hamartian epoiesen*):** This does not mean Christ was turned into an ontological sinner, nor does it mean His moral character was corrupted. It also goes beyond Him merely being a "sin offering" (though the Old Testament sacrificial background of the *chattath* is certainly present). To be "made sin" means that **Christ was judicially identified with our sin**. Our guilt, our law-breaking, and the divine condemnation due to us were legally imputed (reckoned) to Him. He was treated by the Father as if He were the sum total of all our transgressions, bearing the full covenantal curse (cf. Gal 3:13).

#### B. "So that in him we might become the righteousness of God" (The Imputation of Righteousness)
* **"The Righteousness of God" (*dikaiosyne theou*):** In this context, this is not merely an attribute of God (His inherent justice) nor simply His saving activity. Rather, in contrast to "made sin," it refers to a **forensic status**. Just as Christ was judicially constituted as "sin" on the cross, so believers, "in him" (by virtue of union with Christ), are judicially constituted as "righteous" before God.
* **The Mechanism of Imputation:** Imputation is the legal reckoning of a status or value to someone's account. Paul uses this bookkeeping language (*logizomai*) in verse 19 ("not *counting*..."). In the Great Exchange:
  1. **Our sin is imputed to Christ:** He bears our debit, dying our death.
  2. **Christ’s righteousness is imputed to us:** We receive His credit, inheriting His life and standing before the Father.

#### C. Evaluating Alternative Interpretations
Some modern scholars (such as N.T. Wright) have argued that "the righteousness of God" in verse 21 refers to the apostles becoming "embodiments of God’s covenant faithfulness" in their ministry. However, this exegesis falters on the strict grammatical and conceptual parallelism of the verse:

$$\begin{aligned}
\text{He (God)} &\longrightarrow \text{made Him (Christ) to be sin (our status)} \\
\text{so that in Him} &\longrightarrow \text{we might become the righteousness of God (Christ's status)}
\end{aligned}$$

If "made sin" is forensic/representative (Christ representing our sin), then "become the righteousness of God" must also be forensic/representative (believers representing or possessing God’s righteousness in Christ). To shift the meaning of the second clause from a soteriological status (justification) to an apostolic function (ministry) ruptures the rhetorical symmetry of Paul's climax.

### Conclusion

In 2 Corinthians 5:19–21, Paul presents a legally grounded reconciliation. God does not bypass His own righteous standards to achieve peace. Instead, through the Great Exchange of double imputation, God satisfies the demands of His holy wrath against sin by punishing it in His sinless Son (propitiation), thereby legally constituting repentant sinners as perfectly righteous in His sight. Reconciliation (*katallasso*) is the glorious result of this completed forensic transaction.

## Piper's Response
**Prompt:** Explain Romans 5:11 ('we exult in God through our Lord Jesus Christ, through whom we have now received the reconciliation'). What does it mean to 'receive' reconciliation, and how does this relate to Christian Hedonism? Is the ultimate gift of reconciliation actually God Himself?

In Romans 5:11, the Apostle Paul climbs to the absolute peak of the gospel mountain, exclaiming: *“More than that, we also rejoice [exult] in God through our Lord Jesus Christ, through whom we have now received reconciliation.”* 

To understand what it means to “receive” this reconciliation, and how it relates to Christian Hedonism, we must look at the flow of Paul’s argument in Romans 5 and peer into the very heart of the gospel.

---

### 1. What Does It Mean to “Receive” Reconciliation?

Before we can receive reconciliation, we must understand the tragic reality of our natural state. Apart from Christ, we are not merely neutral bystanders; we are “enemies” of God (Romans 5:10), subject to His holy and righteous wrath (Romans 1:18). 

Reconciliation (*katallagē*) is the dramatic intervention of God to remove this hostility. It is the restoration of peace between God and man. 

To “receive” this reconciliation means:
*   **It is an accomplished, objective work:** We did not achieve it; Christ did. By His perfect life and substitutionary death on the cross, He absorbed the wrath of God due to us and purchased our peace. 
*   **It is a gift of free grace:** Just as we do not earn a gift, we do not earn reconciliation. It is received through faith alone (*sola fide*).
*   **It is a subjective reality:** We do not merely look at reconciliation from afar; we "receive" it into our lives. The hostility is cancelled, the adoption papers are signed, and we are brought into a state of warm, secure fellowship with our Creator.

---

### 2. How Does This Relate to Christian Hedonism?

Christian Hedonism is the conviction that **God is most glorified in us when we are most satisfied in Him.** 

Romans 5:11 is one of the most foundational texts for Christian Hedonism in the entire Bible. Notice the word Paul uses: *“we also rejoice in God.”* The Greek word is *kauchōmenoi*, which means to boast, exult, or triumphantly rejoice. 

Paul does not say, “We accept the theological fact of reconciliation.” He says, **“We exult!”** 

This is the language of supreme, soul-satisfying pleasure. Reconciliation is not a cold legal transaction; it is the opening of the floodgates of joy. For the Christian Hedonist, reconciliation is the removal of every obstacle—our sin, our guilt, and God’s righteous wrath—that stands between our thirsty souls and the fountain of living water. We receive reconciliation so that we might plunge into the ocean of joy that is God Himself. 

---

### 3. Is the Ultimate Gift of Reconciliation God Himself?

Yes, absolutely. This is the crucial point that distinguishes saving faith from a mere desire to escape hell.

If you could have a heaven with no sickness, no pain, all your friends, and all the physical pleasures you ever wanted, but God was not there—would you be satisfied? If the answer is yes, you have not received the reconciliation Paul is talking about.

The ultimate gift of the gospel is not forgiveness, though forgiveness is necessary. The ultimate gift is not escape from hell, though that is unspeakably wonderful. The ultimate gift is not even physical healing or streets of gold. **The ultimate gift of the gospel is God.**

Consider how the New Testament authors speak of this:
*   **1 Peter 3:18:** *“For Christ also suffered once for sins, the righteous for the unrighteous, **that he might bring us to God**.”*
*   **Psalm 16:11:** *“In your presence there is fullness of joy; at your right hand are pleasures forevermore.”*

The work of reconciliation is the removal of the barrier so that we can have *God*. To receive reconciliation is to receive the Person with whom we were at war as our Friend, our Father, and our Supreme Treasure. 

When Paul says we “exult in God,” he is showing us that God is both the Author of our reconciliation and the Reward of our reconciliation. The cross of Christ was the price paid so that we could say, forever and ever with infinite delight, **“God is my exceeding joy”** (Psalm 43:4).

## Beale's Response
**Prompt:** How does the concept of 'reconciliation' and 'new creation' in 2 Corinthians 5:17-19 connect to the Old Testament themes of the Temple, the tabernacle, and the eschatological restoration of Eden?

In 2 Corinthians 5:17–19, Paul proclaims that "if anyone is in Christ, he is a new creation" and that "in Christ God was reconciling the world to himself." Within a biblical-theological framework, this concept of "new creation" and "reconciliation" is deeply rooted in the Old Testament trajectories of the Garden of Eden, the Tabernacle, and the Temple. 

In the history of redemption, the Edenic ideal, the sacrificial system of the Tabernacle/Temple, and the hope of eschatological restoration converge in the person and work of Jesus Christ, making Him the ultimate locus of God’s dwelling and the source of cosmic renewal.

### 1. Eden as the Primordial Temple
To understand "new creation," we must begin with the first creation. In Genesis 1–2, the Garden of Eden is depicted not merely as a lush garden, but as the primordial sanctuary—the first Temple. 
* **God’s Presence:** God "walks" (Hebrew *mithallek*) in the Garden (Gen 3:8), the same unique term used for God’s presence dwelling in the Tabernacle (Lev 26:12; 2 Sam 7:6).
* **Priestly Language:** Adam is placed in the Garden to "work it and keep it" (Gen 2:15). The Hebrew words used here (*‘abad* and *shamar*) are the exact terms used later to describe the duties of the Levites guarding and serving in the Tabernacle (Num 3:7–8; 18:5–6).
* **The Fall and Exile:** When humanity sinned, they were expelled from this sanctuary—an exile from the presence of God. The entrance was barred by cherubim (Gen 3:24). This separation created the cosmic need for **reconciliation** (the restoration of fellowship) and **new creation** (the restoration of the ruined cosmos).

### 2. The Tabernacle and Temple as Typological Shadows
Following the expulsion from Eden, God initiated a covenantal framework to dwell among His people again, represented by the Tabernacle and later the Temple. 
* **Copy and Shadow:** The Tabernacle was decorated with Edenic imagery (gold, precious stones, floral motifs, and cherubim woven into the veil) to signal that it was a partial, temporary restoration of Eden.
* **The Barrier of Sin:** However, the Temple also highlighted the unresolved problem of sin. God’s presence dwelt in the Most Holy Place, but it was separated by a thick veil. Only the High Priest could enter, once a year on the Day of Atonement (*Yom Kippur*), with the blood of sacrifices. 
* **The Need for Reconciliation:** The sacrificial system was a provisional means of keeping covenant wrath at bay, but it could not effect final reconciliation or create a "new heart" (Hebrews 10:1–4). The Temple pointed forward to a time when the barrier of sin would be permanently removed.

### 3. Christ as the True Temple and the Accomplishment of Reconciliation
In 2 Corinthians 5:19, Paul writes that **"in Christ God was reconciling the world to himself, not counting their trespasses against them."** 

Jesus is the fulfillment of both the Temple and the sacrificial system:
* **The Dwelling of God:** John 1:14 states that the Word became flesh and "tabernacled" (*eskenosen*) among us. Jesus refers to His own body as the Temple (John 2:19–21).
* **The Ultimate Sacrifice:** On the cross, the veil of the Temple was torn in two (Matt 27:51). This physical tearing signified that through Christ’s substitutionary death, the barrier between God and man was demolished. 
* **Propitiation:** God reconciled us to Himself by "not counting our trespasses against us" but instead counting them to Christ (2 Cor 5:21). Christ’s body is the ultimate "mercy seat" (Romans 3:25)—the place where holy justice and divine mercy meet, achieving true reconciliation.

### 4. Union with Christ: The Believer as the New Temple and New Creation
In 2 Corinthians 5:17, Paul declares, "if anyone is **in Christ**, he is a new creation." This is grounded in the doctrine of *union with Christ*. Because believers are united to Christ by faith through the Holy Spirit, what is true of Christ becomes true of them.

* **The Eschatological Temple:** Since Christ is the True Temple, those who are "in Christ" corporately become the Temple of the living God. Paul explicitly makes this connection later in the same letter: *"For we are the temple of the living God; as God said, 'I will make my dwelling among them and walk among them'"* (2 Cor 6:16, quoting Lev 26:12 and Ezek 37:27).
* **The Indwelling Spirit:** In the Old Testament, the glory of God filled the Tabernacle and Temple (Exod 40:34; 1 Kings 8:10–11). In the New Covenant, the Holy Spirit indwells the believer and the Church (1 Cor 3:16; 6:19). This indwelling is the "guarantee" (*arrabon*) of the final eschatological restoration (2 Cor 1:22; 5:5).
* **The Restoration of Eden:** The "new creation" is the inauguration of the New Covenant promises prophesied by Isaiah (Isa 65:17; 66:22) and Ezekiel (Ezek 36:26–27). The old order of sin, death, and exile (the "old things" of 2 Cor 5:17) has passed away in Christ’s death. The "new things"—the spiritual resurrection, the law written on the heart, and immediate access to God—have arrived.

### Conclusion
The themes of Temple, Tabernacle, and Eden culminate beautifully in 2 Corinthians 5:17–19. 
Through the work of Christ, God has bypassed the physical stone Temple in Jerusalem to establish a living, spiritual Temple. Believers, reconciled to God through the death of Christ, are brought back into the "Garden" of God’s intimate presence. We are made participants in the "new creation," serving as royal priests (the ministry of reconciliation) until the day when the New Jerusalem descends, of which John writes: *"I saw no temple in the city, for its temple is the Lord God the Almighty and the Lamb"* (Revelation 21:22).
